Open example:

A strangely formative moment in my political consciousness occurred while watching the 2012 republican national convention with my college housemates. They were nominating Romney to run against an incumbant Obama, and since Romney had absolutely dominated the primaries, the convention was free to spend the entirey of their effort on pagentry.

Prior to watching, my housemates and I had scribbled down rules for a drinking game (such was life at a Wisconsin liberal arts college). I have long since forgotten the rules, but they were along the lines of "drink everytime someone pushes for school choice", "drink everytime someone mentions the sanctity of marriage" or "drink everytime someone blames Obama for the 2008 bank bailouts (which happened under Bush but fiscal hawks on the right had begun blaming Obama)".

What I do remember is that by the second or third speaker it was clear that our rules were basically worthless. There weren't significant policy proposals. Instead, speaker after speaker said the same thing: "Yes, we built it".

The quote line was in response to Obama saying "you didn't build that"



During the 2012 presidential campaign, Obama said "Somebody helped to create this unbelievable American system that we have that allowed you to thrive. Somebody invested in roads and bridges. If you've got a business, you didn't build that.". In response, the Republican National Convention paraded out entrepeneur after entrepeneur who proudly exclaimed "I built that". This sticks out in my mind because it was the first time I was sure that politicians were acting in bad faith (instead of just being full of bullshit). But what exactly was being done in bad faith?


-----

The space for uncharitable interpretation is clear when you take the offending sentence on its own.

> If you've got a business, you didn't build that.

It looks like "that" refers to the business. 


There are really two possible offenses here, committed by different people. 

The first offense is the only one necessarily done with bad faith. In the context of the entire passage, it is incredibly clear that Obama's "that" refers to infrastructure. There is certainly some space to quibble on the point (my taxes paid for the road, etc.) but that's far too subtle of a point to arrange the RNC around. Instead, someone somewhere in the RNC or greater conservative media ecosystem recognized the opportunity for interpreting the sentence in a way that makes Obama look bad. They recognized the opportunity created by stripping the sentence of context and emphasizing the harmful interpretation.

Call this *strategic uncharitability*. This is the act of recognizing that a sentence can be interpreted in a way that makes what the speaker says is false, immoral, or otherwise damaging to them. 

I do not think this was in the minds of most of the speakers in the RNC. I remember being horrified by how emotional entrepreneuers got when they defended their legacy. The RNC's message wouldn't have landed if people had stopped to think *wait isn't it kind of absurd that a speaker as charismatic as Obama had said something so obviously politically toxic?* 

Call this *hostile interpretation*.



Context collapse certianly helped








Performative Uncharitability: When you purposefully misinterpret something for the purpose of scoring points.

Hostile reads/interpretation: When, due to beliefs about the speaker, you interpret something in the worst possible light.
